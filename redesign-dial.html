§2Seven replacements

PITCH I

The index drawer — the desk gets the drawer unit it keeps talking about

legiblediegetic
all eleven doors, one action each · the room you are standing in is marked in place, not opened in a popup

the pitch

The journal's owner keeps an index. It has been in his dialogue since the beginning — “an index is kindness aimed at your future self” — while the desktop shipped a carousel. This pitch takes him at his word: the desk gets the drawer unit, a rail of hand-cut cards, one per door, with the traveller's name and the room's place printed on each.

Each card carries the room's phosphor as a spine stripe and a glyph — a lamp, allowed to be as chromatic as it likes — and prints the name in ink on paper. That single split is the whole fix for the contrast failure: buddy reads in dark ink on card stock no matter how dark sigil's lamp is.

The drawer is also the honest answer to where is here. The card for the room you are standing in slides up proud of the rest, the way a pulled card does. No badge, no popup, no secret.

how it clears the floor

  • D1 · D2 — eleven cards, one press each, all visible at 1280 and at 439 (the cards wrap; nothing is ever behind anything).
  • D3 — names print in ink on card stock; the room ink only stripes the card's edge and draws the glyph.
  • D4 — 46px cards, 15px names, 11px place lines, 122px minimum width.
  • D5 — the drawer sits on the desk below the machine. It never covers the tube; the index popup disappears from the code.
  • D6 — the here-card and the lit edge are the memory. Water-stained once you have been: the card for a visited room keeps a faint thumb-smudge.
  • not a library — the drawer's timber, its rail, its paper and its ink are the study's, the same materials rooms.data.js already declares for riason: indexed vellum, brass clasps, oak, ink.
PITCH II

The house, from the yard — the menu is the building's own face

legiblediegeticthe mind
the glasshouse · the paint store · the yard
BELOW THE BOARDS
hover or tab a window: it lights and names its room and its place · nothing covers the tube

the pitch

The machine is a house. It has always been a house — rooms.data.js registers every room by its place: the study, the bathroom, the basement, the observatory, the sleeping nook, the stacks, the play room. The dial throws that away and shows an alphabetical list. This pitch uses it: the desktop's menu becomes the building's own face, ten lit windows arranged the way the rooms actually stand, basement below a dashed board line, glasshouse and yard under the roof.

Point at a window and it lights, naming the room and its place. The light is the traveller's phosphor — so the facade is coloured by the house, room by room, which no list can do. Press it and you are in.

This is the version of the pitch that answers the brief most literally. A haunted computer that exists inside your mind does not hand you a nav bar — it hands you a plan of itself, and lets you notice which windows you have left dark.

how it clears the floor

  • D1 · D2 — ten windows, one press, all on screen. The unvisited ones read at a lower ink but never below the floor, so the house reads as a house on a fresh machine, not as a grid of zeroes (covenant rule 4's empty condition).
  • D3 — the name is #f4ead0 ink on the window's own dark glass; the phosphor is the light behind it, at 16% when idle and 85% when lit.
  • D4 — every window is a 52px-minimum target with a 13px name; the place line is 9.5px and only ever appears on the window you are on, so it never has to be readable in a crowd.
  • D5 — the facade replaces the 26%-height track entirely. There is no strip, no band, no border across the traveller's screen: the menu is the wall the machine stands against.
  • honest limit — the layout is authored furniture, not a computed floor plan. If a room moves, someone redraws it; the pitch treats that as the point, not the cost.
PITCH III

The tube rack — ten phosphor slots, stamped in bakelite

legiblediegeticcheapest to build
the lit slot is the room you were last in · every slot is stamped with its own grid reference

the pitch

Settings already carries a maintenance panel with a VFD slot display; the machine's own hardware register is established. This pitch extends it to the desk: a rack of eleven phosphor slots, each a tube with the room's name stamped into its base and a plate reference beside it — S1, B1, O3, Y5.

The names are stamped, not lit, so the label is legible whether the tube is burning or dead — which is exactly what a real rack does, and exactly what the current dial fails to do. The phosphor lives in the glass, behind the stamp. The selected slot glows and its base brightens.

The grid references are the covenant's oddly precise number and its cross-reference to a document you will never see, in one stroke: Y5 means something to whoever wired this machine, and you are not going to find out what.

how it clears the floor

  • D3 — the stamp is #e2cca0 on bakelite, identical lit or dark. The phosphor never touches the text.
  • D4 — slots are 52px of glass plus a 24px base, 64px minimum width; the whole rack is auto-fit, so it reflows from eleven columns to three without ever crushing a label (the truncation in the mockup is a text-overflow guard, not the design).
  • D2 — no carousel, no popup: the rack is the index.
  • cheapest to build — this is the one that reuses the most. The tile → slot rename is mechanical, state.visited already drives the lit slot, and the arrow keys that cycle the dial become left/right along the rack. It could ship in a day.
  • its honest weakness — a rack is a rack; it is an appliance, not a house. It is the least haunted of the five and the most legible. If the goal is "the machine's furniture", this is the safe door.
PITCH IV

The worn three — a door that has noticed your hand

hauntedlegiblediegetic
the three doors your hand actually wears · the order is yours, not an alphabet's

the pitch

Every door in this machine already remembers you: the journal thickens its spine, the garden waters its pot on each visit, the trash grows graves. The one piece of furniture that refuses to show that is the menu — it holds a fixed order and hides its memory in a small badge.

The worn three makes the memory the menu. The desk keeps three slots for the rooms you have actually been in, most recent first, each one worn where you press it — a lifted patina dish, the same tell the covenant asks of a well-used prop. Under them, one handle opens the drawer with the other eight.

And because the machine lives in your mind, it is allowed to say so once, in the traveller's voice, in the register the dial's opinion line already uses: “three days, vanir's room. the boards are still wet.” The menu becomes a mirror rather than a list.

how it clears the floor

  • D1 · D2 — three doors at one press, eight more at two. This is the one pitch here that does not meet D1 for the whole house, and it says so: its claim is that the house you actually use is three rooms, and the other eight are a drawer away by design.
  • D3 — 15px names in #f4ead0 on the slot's own dark metal, phosphor only as a lamp behind.
  • D4 — 60px slots, 44px drawer handle.
  • D6 — this pitch is D6. Recency is the order; the wear is the record; the drawer is where the rest of the house waits, patiently, unresentful.
  • the risk — a menu that reorders itself is a menu you cannot memorise. The mitigation is the fix: the slots never move while you are on the desk — the order settles when you come back, the way a room looks different when you return to it.
PITCH V

The ring — a dial that is actually radial, and an instrument it can be turned

diegeticthe mindlegible at the plate
buddy sigil · stone & seals 27 ENGRAVINGS · 36° DETENTS · REV. C
turn · click · the engraving under the pointer reads on the plate
ten engravings, 36° apart, one pointer · the plate below states the room, its traveller and its place

the pitch

The file is called dial.js, the stylesheet says “radial-style”, and the thing that renders is a row of three plaques. Meanwhile the sea already has a brass tide clock with a knurled bezel, and the covenant's material register is full of instruments. So: make the ring.

A brass azimuth ring around the tube, ten engravings at true angles, a knurled bezel you turn with a drag or the arrow keys, detented to 36° so an engraving always sits under the pointer — no half-word ever comes to rest. The pointer drops a name plate: the room, the traveller, the place, all in printed ink on brass, at a size you can read from where you are sitting.

This is the reading that takes the word dial seriously and gives the desktop an object instead of a control: the machine becomes an instrument, and choosing a room becomes tuning it. It is also the pitch with the strongest claim to the brief — an instrument is exactly what a haunted machine would be.

One decision the mockup makes and the build must keep: the engravings are cut upright, each counter-rotated to meet the eye square. Physically dishonest by a hair, and the right call — an engraving you have to tilt your head to read is the same failure as a label printed in a dark lamp.

how it clears the floor

  • D3 — nothing is read off the ring itself. The engravings are placeholders for the eye; the name plate carries the name in ink, and the plate is the only text that has to be legible.
  • D4 — the plate is a 19px name on brass; each engraving's own hit target is a 44px sector at its angle, so the ring is not just turnable, it is pressable.
  • D5 — the ring sits around the tube's own bezel rather than under it, so it costs the screen nothing. It is the only pitch here that adds no new furniture at all: it is the monitor's existing edge, re-cut.
  • D2, honestly — ten engravings around a ring are all present but not all equally readable; the plate is what makes it D2-compliant. A traveller should be able to Tab the ring and read the plate at each stop, and this pitch only works if that traversal is built.
  • the reduced-motion test — the bezel must not ease between detents under prefers-reduced-motion; it snaps, the plate changes, and the function is intact. A ring that must be turned is a ring that must be snap-turnable.
PITCH VI

The phosphor bank — one nixie door, soldered into the CRT

diegeticlegiblethe mind
cold · the cathodes are still visible, so the door is still findable
spent · a room you have been through keeps a settled light
burning · the door you are standing in, in its own phosphor
struck · the casting lock, cut through the plate
the component at roughly 3× · plate above, collar and envelope below · focus is a lit bezel around the plate

the pitch

One door, one component, wired into the machine's face. An enamel-filled engraved plate carries the name; below it a Bakelite collar with two slotted screws holds a nixie envelope — domed glass, a mica spacer, the anode mesh, one cathode wire — and the cathode burns in that room's own phosphor.

The split is the whole point, and it is the fix for everything in §0. The glow is the room; the name is enamel. The plate is real steel, the letterforms are cut into it and filled, and they read identically whether the tube is cold, spent, or burning. The bulb identifies and reports state; it never carries information on its own.

And a cold tube is not a hidden door. A real nixie shows you its cathodes when it is off — you can count the numerals through the glass with the power cut. Here, cold, you see the wire and the ember, the plate reads the name, and the door is exactly as findable as it was lit. That is what the current dial's ··· all rooms hint should have been.

Why enamel-filled, honestly: bare engraved metal — dark cut letters on a mid-tone panel — measures 1.9:1. The relief that makes an engraving legible in your hand is a lighting effect that a flat screen cannot reproduce. Machine nameplates have always solved this the same way: cut the letters, fill them with enamel. So the plate is dark steel and the engraving is cream, which is both truer to the object and legible.

how it clears the floor

  • D3 — the engraved plate is enamel on steel, measured on the plate itself, and it does not move when the state changes.
  • D4 — in the chin each door is 74×46 with its name at 11px; the component study above is roughly 3× that. The collar's screws are decoration and are never the target.
  • D6 — four states, all visible at once and none of them a hover: cold, spent, burning, struck. The bank reads as a record of your own traffic before you touch anything.
  • F6 consent — under reduced motion the tube does not fade; it switches. The ember, the bloom and the halo are all present in both worlds.
  • the carving — the small etched mark on the collar's shoulder is the room's own glyph (see below). It is a scratch on a fitting, not a control, and it is not in the tab order.

And the twelve carvings move home, which is what frees the bezel

The bank needs the machine's face, and the face is already furnished: styles/bezel.css places twelve carvings around the bezel — four along the top, two on each side, four along the bottom, twenty-two pixels each, and every one of them in the tab order. The sweep flagged those twenty-two-pixel targets. It was right to, and the fix is not to enlarge them.

Read src/carvings.js and they are not navigation at all: each carving is bound to a theme id and lights when that traveller's skin is live. They are marks — evidence of who has been here — and the covenant already says where evidence lives. Not on the machine's face as buttons: in the room behind the CRT, on that room's own named materials. So they go to the wall, the board, the sill, the shelf, the pool and the boards — each on the material it belongs to — and the whole bezel comes free for the bank.

PITCH VII

The legend bar — keys standing proud, the room's name cut into the case

diegeticlegiblethe mind
cold · the key stands up on its stem, the tube is dark, and the cathode wire still reads through the glass
spent · you have been through, and the tube keeps a settled flame
latched · the cap has sunk onto its detent and covered its own stem. the case has not moved and neither has the name
blanking plug · the way is cut into the case, the switch behind it was never wired, and the legend is struck
the column at roughly 2.3× · same beige plastic as the case, a dished face you can see over the top edge, and a post you can see at rest · the name is milled into the CASE, never on the key

the pitch

The machine's forehead is 63px tall and has never held anything but twelve 22px carvings, which is why the sweep flagged them: four along the top, in the tab order, unreadable and un-hittable. Measured off the live desktop.html at 1280×800: the machine runs 920×718 at (27, 41), the glass starts at y=108, so that head is 63px of case. The carvings move home (above). What is left is a strip the machine has been carrying for nothing.

Two moves, both small. First the head comes down to 40px. That hands 23px back to the glass — 787×567 becomes 787×590 — and pays for the whole index out of the case's own fat. Second, the keys do not sit in wells; they stand up. Each is a tall cap in the same beige plastic as the case, dished on top, flared at the skirt, standing 30px proud of the machine's top edge with 8px of travel. The keys live outside the case, so they cost the head nothing; the head only has to hold the tube and the name.

Why they must be tall, and why they must be the case's own colour. A short key sunk in a hole is a switch; a tall one you can see over is furniture. Standing 30px proud with a dished face means the height itself carries the news — you can count the keys and see which one is down from the far side of the room, before you can read a single letter. And keeping them the machine's own beige is what stops them reading as a control panel bolted onto a monitor. Nothing here is a new material. The keys are the case, cut up and stood on end.

And the name is engraved in the case, not on the key. A cap is a moving part, and a moving part is the worst possible home for the one thing you have to read. That is precisely how the dial fails today: the tile you are on is the least legible thing on the desktop, measured at 2.34:1 in §0. This is the structural version of that fix. The moving part is the state; the case is the name. Dark fill in light plastic measures 9.98:1 at its worst gradient stop, and it reads identically cold, latched or struck, because the letters are in the machine and the key is a separate object in front of them.

The tube lies in a recessed channel directly above the legend, dark-ground so that even a pale phosphor reads against it. Cold it shows its cathode wire. Lit it burns in that room's own colour and spills onto the plate below — so the light reveals the legend instead of being the legend. A lit door's name gains contrast; it never borrows the colour it is read by.

It is still an interlock bank: pressing a key mechanically releases the last one, so the room you are standing in is the one sitting low. Depth is the address, the tube is the room, and the case is the name — three channels, none of them load-bearing on another, and you can read where you are from across the room with nothing hovered.

how it clears the floor

  • D1 · one action — eleven keys, eleven names, one press each. Nothing is behind a hold, nothing behind an arrow.
  • D2 · all of it visible — all eleven names are engraved in the case and readable at 1280 with the power off. At 439 the legends fall to three letters, and that is a real loss this pitch admits to below.
  • D3 · lamp vs. ink — the name is engraved in the case and the key is a separate object; the lamp is a third thing again, sitting above the legend. Nothing moves the letters and nothing colours them.
  • D4 · a size you can hold — each key's button is the whole column: 86×63 at 1280, since cap, channel and legend are all inside it. That is well past 44×44 with room for a real 5px travel.
  • D5 · the room wins — the bar is on the case, and the head already existed. At 1280 this pitch hands the glass 23px back instead of taking a band from it; the liberchat tube stands to the machine's right and never crosses the bar.
  • D6 · it remembers you — the remembered room is the key sitting low, a spent room is a tube at low flame. Both read before you touch anything, without a hover. This is the pitch's whole argument in one line: the dial knew where you were and hid it in a badge; the case says it in relief.
  • the sound — src/sound.js already ships thunk(), which is exactly the noise a key landing on a detent makes. The click is never the information: the key's depth and the tube's colour both survive with the sound off.
  • the drawing prop — the channel's lower lip catches a 0.3px highlight off the plastic, the legend's shadow falls one pixel down and right, and the cap is genuinely concave. Nothing floats: every part is a fitting in a hole.

THE THREE THINGS THIS SETTLES

1 · the sidecar never covers the menu. From 900px up the tube stands entirely to the right of the machine — at 1280 the machine ends at x=947 and the sidecar begins at x=957. Below 900px it stops fitting and drops to the bottom-left, still below the dial's band. Your right-hand worry is real, but it is not an overlap: it is that the sidecar takes the desk.

2 · the desk is not a place. 27px of gutter on the left and 51px on the right at 1280, 12px at 1024, nothing at all below 900. Any version of these pitches that means “a drawer standing beside the machine” is dead on arrival. “Outside the machine” has to mean on the machine.

3 · the bezel is the room you were looking for. A uniform 67px frame at 1280 — 67px cheeks running the full 690px, a 920×67 head, a 920×56 chin. It costs the glass nothing, it is never scrolled and never covered, and today the dial is instead eating 145px of the 567px glass — a quarter of every room's screen.

The same bank, if you would rather have it vertical

The chin is the mount I would choose — a full name fits at 11px and the badges live there already. But the cheek is a real option now that the carvings have moved out, and it is the taller one: a 49px column carries all eleven doors at 49 × 50 each with abbreviated plates, the way a patch bay or a breaker panel labels its ways. Two letters are all the maker needed; the full name appears on the glass when the door is lit.

The cheek strip at true size — 49px wide, eleven doors, 50px each. This is the mount that survives best between 900px and 1024px, where the chin narrows to 45px but the cheek keeps its full 690px of height. It is also the one that puts the machine's doors where a real machine's switches go, down the left frame where your hand already rests.

The abbreviated plate is the diegetic register doing real work: BUD, JRN, SEA are what a maker stencils on a panel, not what a designer writes in a mockup. Three characters at 10px in 41px of plate is comfortable; divination at 11px in the chin is just as comfortable. Both are legible; only one of them can also be read from across the room.

And the 439px desktop, at true scale — where the bezel stops being an option

The machine is 96vw here, the bezel is 22px, and the sidecar's fit test fails at 900px so it drops to the bottom-left below the machine. A bezel rail cannot hold a 44px key in 22px of frame. So at this size the answer is not a rail — it is one always-visible door: the chin carries a single 44px tab naming the room you are standing in, which opens the drawer over the glass. That is 44px instead of the 69px band the carousel takes now, and the current room is still named on the machine at all times.

THE REVISED RECOMMENDATION — MOUNT IT ON THE MACHINE

Take your own steer and the measurements together and the answer is not “a menu inside the screen”. It is the machine's own frame carrying the machine's own doors.

the chin (920 × 56) carries the drawer rail — pitch I or III: eleven doors in the strip where the maker's badge already sits.

the left cheek (67 × 690) carries the wear — pitch IV's three worn slots and their drawer, running down the frame the way a real machine keeps its switches.

the glass (787 × 567) gets all 145px back, and pitch II lives there as the desktop's wallpaper rather than as furniture at all — the house as the traveller's own backdrop, which is what a facade should be.

Result: a full quarter of every room's screen returned, the menu permanently visible on the body of the machine instead of stacked on the bottom of the picture, nothing competing with the tube for the desk — and one honest fallback at 439, where the bezel gives out and a single 44px tab names where you are.

The judgements are mine; the numbers are measurable and the mockups above are the evidence.

WHAT I AM NOT PROPOSINGI am not proposing a component library. The drawer, the facade and the rack share one thing only: a name printed in ink, a phosphor that is only a lamp, and 44px of surface you can press. The drawer is oak and card stock; the facade is a building; the rack is bakelite and glass. Nothing here could be lifted from one of these into another unchanged — which is the covenant's actual test.
